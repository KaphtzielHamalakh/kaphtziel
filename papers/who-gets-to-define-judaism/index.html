Who Gets to Define Judaism?

Responding to Morris Jastrow, Shaul Magid, and the Argument Against Political Zionism

Reading my friend and teacher Shaul Magid’s October 1, 2026 Substack essay, “Morris Jastrow and the Case Against Political Zionism,” I found myself arguing with Jastrow’s account of Judaism almost from the beginning. Shaul recovers a Jewish scholar whose opposition to political Zionism rested partly on his conviction that Judaism had developed beyond its ancient national existence into a universal religion. I don’t recognize the fullness of Jewish life in that description, and I question the authority it claims over our political choices.1

Shaul expressly disclaims agreement or disagreement with Jastrow’s approach; he wants to recover earlier debates so that we can consider Jewish possibilities that contemporary arguments often exclude. He does endorse one observation later in the essay: a Jewish state enabled by colonial powers would be perceived by the Muslim population as an extension of European imperialism and would fail to provide a democratic solution to the postwar problem. I take that observation seriously without attributing Jastrow’s entire position to Shaul.

I have trouble with the movement from a definition of Judaism to a prohibition on Jewish statehood. Jews have carried forms of collective belonging through centuries of dispersion, and those relationships give us reasons to seek political self-determination. Defending that pursuit also requires us to account for the lives and freedoms of Palestinians. Neither obligation disappears when we find a definition of Judaism we prefer.

Is Judaism a religion?

In his 1919 book, Zionism and the Future of Palestine, Jastrow describes Judaism as emerging “as the butterfly out of the chrysalis of Hebrew nationalism.” Ancient national existence becomes the casing from which a universal religious life emerges; a return to nationalism would reverse that development. The image gives religious universalism the status of an achievement and treats the national past as something Judaism has outgrown.2

Jews preserved law, language, kinship, collective memory, institutions, obligations to one another, and attachment to a particular land throughout their dispersion. Much of this survived through religious practice, which makes it difficult to draw the boundary Jastrow’s argument requires. When we remember leaving Egypt at the seder, we place ourselves within the experience of a people. When we pray for Jerusalem, geography, memory, and religious hope belong to the same act. Describing these practices as religious hardly exhausts their meaning.

Jastrow does acknowledge shared tradition, including among Jews who have ceased to be religious. He distinguishes nationality from a political nation and considers a Palestine in which several nationalities share a state. We should grant him that understanding of collective identity when we criticize his conclusions.2

Even so, why should the loss of ancient sovereignty determine the permissible future of a people? Changes in language, institutions, and geography do not ordinarily disqualify other peoples from considering new political arrangements. Jastrow needs to explain why Judaism’s development in exile forecloses a national future, especially when the traditions he recognizes have continued to sustain a collective Jewish life.

An ancient kingdom cannot serve as a deed to modern territory, and establishing that Jews remain a people does not establish the justice of every arrangement pursued in their name. It does, however, require us to argue about those arrangements on their merits. A claim about Judaism’s religious essence cannot settle questions of citizenship, territory, or political rights before that argument begins.

Judaism and the emancipation bargain

I find it deeply troubling when Judaism must first fit a Western definition of religion to become intelligible. Jews are asked to divide their inherited life into categories developed within another history, then accept the resulting account as a description of what they have always been.

European emancipation gave this pressure the force of public policy. Jews gained rights as individual citizens while the legitimacy of a distinct Jewish corporate or national existence came under suspicion. They could practice their religion within a civic order whose authorities reserved the power to decide which Jewish institutions and loyalties were compatible with citizenship.

French Jewish emancipation reached completion in 1791, before Napoleon came to power. In 1806 he summoned Jewish notables to answer questions about marriage, rabbinic authority, civic loyalty, and their relationship to other French people; the Grand Sanhedrin followed in 1807. The representatives affirmed France as their country and recognized the authority of its civil law.3

They substantially accepted the bargain. Nevertheless, Napoleon’s discriminatory decree of 1808 imposed restrictions on Jewish commerce and settlement, with particular exceptions. Jews who had declared their civic loyalty could still be singled out for treatment that other citizens did not have to endure.3

If we call this a Faustian bargain, we should specify the price: the demand that Jews surrender authority over their collective self-description in exchange for equality. We should also remember what equality offered people emerging from exclusion. Jews who embraced emancipation pursued real freedoms, often with courage, and many believed sincerely in the religious universalism through which they understood their citizenship. Their convictions cannot all be dismissed as capitulation.

Jewish theology, of course, long preceded these encounters with the modern state. My objection concerns the insistence that religion, culture, and politics must be separated according to a particular European model, even when that separation distorts the life it is supposed to explain. I regard that insistence as cultural imperialism. It permits the state’s categories to determine which forms of Jewish belonging are acceptable, then treats Jewish resistance as evidence of an inability to become modern.

Kaplan’s evolving religious civilization

Mordecai Kaplan’s account of Judaism as the evolving religious civilization of the Jewish people comes much closer to the life I recognize. In Judaism as a Civilization, he writes that Judaism “includes that nexus of history, literature, language, social organization,” continuing with communal standards, ideals, and aesthetic values.4

Kaplan also approvingly quotes Israel Friedlaender’s call to “break the narrow frame of a creed” and recover Judaism’s encompassing cultural life. That passage locates the difficulty in the categories used to describe Jewish existence: preserving a set of beliefs and ceremonies would leave much of that existence unaccounted for.4

Religious practice belongs within this account alongside a language learned from a grandparent, a communal institution, a melody, and the responsibility one Jew feels for another. Changes in an individual’s beliefs need not dissolve those relationships. Converts enter this collective life too, which is one reason ancestry alone cannot adequately describe membership in the Jewish people.

Calling the civilization evolving allows for the possibility that institutions once essential to Jewish survival may need to change. We can argue over those changes without treating every departure from an inherited arrangement as a departure from Judaism. That includes arguing over the state: what it enables Jews to preserve or create, which obligations it helps them fulfill, and which commitments its pursuit of power may damage.

Kaplan’s book appeared in 1934, after Jastrow’s death in 1921, so it belongs to a later stage of this debate. Its conception of Judaism does not establish that a civilization requires sovereignty. Jewish life can flourish across borders, while a state can support cultural development or subordinate it to national policy. Kaplan gives us grounds to evaluate those possibilities by their consequences for Jewish life, including aspects of that life that a narrower religious definition leaves out.

Richard Gottheil: religion sustained by a people

Jastrow’s contemporaries had already developed alternatives to his account of Jewish belonging. The works discussed here appeared before his 1919 book; they establish the competing arguments available in his own time.

Richard Gottheil, whom Shaul mentions, is a particularly revealing comparison. Like Jastrow, he was a scholar of Semitic languages and the son of a prominent rabbi. He taught at Columbia and became president of the Federation of American Zionists. Similar scholarly formation and family backgrounds led these men to sharply different conclusions about Jewish national life.5

In Zionism, published in 1914, Gottheil acknowledges the sincerity of the reformers who sought to adapt Judaism to emancipation. He argues that they hoped to save Jewish religion at the expense of nationality and race, failing to recognize those elements as “the chief bulwarks of the religion.” His racial vocabulary belongs to a framework we should question. His historical objection remains: the religion depended on forms of collective life that the reformers’ definition obscured.5

Gottheil also reproduces the Seventh Zionist Congress’s reaffirmation of the Basel program: “The establishment of a legally-secured, publicly-recognized home for the Jewish people in Palestine.” These are the movement’s words. His explanation emphasizes legal security and international recognition because settlement alone could reproduce the insecurity Jews already experienced elsewhere.5

We should be careful about what he meant by that home. Gottheil considered autonomy under a powerful liberal sponsor potentially more secure than complete independence. His Zionism cannot simply be equated with a later program of unrestricted sovereignty. Yet his argument requires Jastrow to explain why Jewish national institutions would threaten a religion whose survival had depended upon communal solidarity. I would like to see Shaul develop that disagreement between these two scholars as fully as he develops Jastrow’s objections.

Simon Dubnow: nationality without a territorial state

Dubnow complicates the argument further because he rejected territorial Zionism while insisting that Jews remained a nation. In Jewish History, he writes, “Bereft of country and dispersed as it is, the Jewish nation lives.” The absence of sovereignty had changed the forms of Jewish national existence without extinguishing it.6

In “The Doctrine of Jewish Nationalism,” he describes a people “deprived of geographical boundaries,” sustained through communal institutions across the diaspora. His conclusion is explicit: “In place of political autonomy it created for itself social and cultural autonomy.” Schools, communal government, historical memory, and cultural creation could carry national life beyond the borders of a state.6

Dubnow’s secular account still recognized religion as an integral part of Jewish national culture. Secularization could broaden participation in the people without requiring the destruction of its religious inheritance. His program sought recognized collective rights in the countries where Jews lived, extending emancipation beyond the individual citizen to the community’s ability to organize its own life.

I find his account of Jewish belonging far more convincing than Jastrow’s reduction of nationality to a stage Judaism had outgrown. I am less persuaded that cultural autonomy provides sufficient protection against a hostile government. But I cannot use Dubnow to support Jewish peoplehood and then ignore his challenge to my own Zionism. If collective Jewish life could endure without sovereignty, a defense of sovereignty has to explain which vulnerabilities make it necessary and which forms of diaspora life remain worth preserving.

Ahad Ha’am: national renewal and the conduct of settlers

Ahad Ha’am gives us a way to dispute Jastrow’s religious premise while questioning the priorities of political Zionism. In “The Jewish State and the Jewish Problem” in 1897, he envisioned a cultural center in Palestine whose influence would reach Jewish communities throughout the diaspora. He described it as “the centre of the nation,” nourishing a people whose future would continue to extend beyond the land.7

He feared that the pursuit of “material power and political dominion” could estrange Jews from the culture that gave their national aspirations a purpose. He allowed for eventual statehood, developed through a prior renewal of Jewish life, while disputing the expectation that a state would solve Jewish poverty, deliver tranquility, or secure national glory. Education, language, literature, and ethical commitments could not be postponed until after political success.7

His criticism also reached the treatment of Arabs. In his 1891 essay “A Truth from Eretz Yisrael,” he challenged the fantasy of an empty land and condemned Jewish settlers’ cruelty toward its inhabitants. He urged Jews to deal with them “with love and respect” and “with justice and good judgment.” Those words belong to a longer warning that abuse would produce anger and resistance.7

His essay contains stereotypes and assumptions about Arabs that deserve criticism themselves; it would be misleading to recruit him as an uncomplicated spokesman for present-day egalitarianism. Even so, he confronted the moral danger of settlers acquiring power over people whose lives they had failed to understand.

I can recognize Jewish national renewal in this account without endorsing every feature of Herzl’s political program. I also find an obligation that survives any disagreement over the path to statehood: a movement pursuing Jewish freedom has to answer for its treatment of the people living alongside it. Criticism of that conduct can arise from within a commitment to Jewish national life.

Louis Brandeis: Jewish loyalty and American citizenship

Brandeis challenged the assumption that national Jewish attachment compromised civic belonging. In his 1915 address, “The Jewish Problem: How to Solve It,” he declares, “Let no American imagine that Zionism is inconsistent with Patriotism.” He then states, “There is no inconsistency between loyalty to America and loyalty to Jewry.”8

He understood Americans as already participating in several relationships of loyalty: to their country, their locality, their families, and their associations. He invoked Irish Americans’ support for home rule as an example of a commitment abroad that could enrich citizenship at home. Supporting Jewish settlement did not require an American Jew to plan to leave America.

That argument answers the emancipation demand that Jews surrender national identification as proof of civic reliability. A Jew could share fully in American political life while helping sustain another center of Jewish life elsewhere. Whether particular commitments conflict remains a question about their content, rather than something established simply by counting the communities to which a person belongs.

As Shaul observes, the address does not specify a complete constitutional arrangement for Palestine. Brandeis’s defense of American Jewish belonging therefore leaves unresolved the political rights of Palestine’s Arab inhabitants. We can accept his rejection of an automatic conflict of loyalties while demanding an adequate answer to that other question.

From Pittsburgh to Columbus

The Pittsburgh Platform of 1885 declares, “We consider ourselves no longer a nation, but a religious community,” and rejects an expectation of return to Palestine or restoration of the laws concerning a Jewish state. Its authors understood emancipation and religious universalism as grounds for relinquishing national restoration. This was an influential interpretation within Jewish life, with the authority of a movement behind it.9

By 1937, the Columbus Platform affirmed an obligation to assist Palestine’s development “as a Jewish homeland” and envisioned “a haven of refuge for the oppressed,” alongside a center of Jewish culture and spiritual life. It recognized the bonds of common history and the loyalty of Jews estranged from religious tradition, while continuing to give religion a foundational place in Jewish peoplehood. It also affirmed the responsibilities of citizenship wherever Jews lived.10

Reform Judaism had revised a central implication of its earlier platform amid Nazi persecution, before the Holocaust. Columbus’s homeland was not a fully specified sovereign state, and its theology remained different from Kaplan’s. The movement could nevertheless reconsider its opposition to a national home without abandoning its religious commitments. The change undermines any claim that Jewish religious universalism carried one necessary political conclusion, fixed for all time.

Settlement, refuge, and sovereignty

Jastrow distinguishes religious, economic, and political Zionism, welcoming settlement that relieves hardship while rejecting a Jewish state. His religious category concerns attachment to the land and restoration understood through faith; it should not be confused with every movement called Religious Zionism today.2

My initial objection was that economic Zionism depended on political membership in the land where Jews were settling. That needs qualification, since Jews did establish farms, schools, businesses, and communal institutions under another sovereign authority. A Jewish state was never a logical prerequisite for such activity.

The dependence becomes clearer when we ask who can authorize the admission of a family fleeing persecution and protect its right to remain. A community may have money, employment, and housing to offer while lacking any authority over immigration policy. Its capacity to provide refuge then depends on decisions made by a government with other priorities.

Citizenship, enforceable minority protections, and autonomy over communal affairs could address some of this vulnerability, including within a shared state. Anyone proposing those arrangements would need to explain what happens when the governing majority withdraws its support or an international guarantor declines to enforce its promises.

Sovereignty offers greater control over those decisions, though it cannot eliminate dependence on allies, trade, resources, or events elsewhere. The case for Jewish sovereignty rests partly on that additional capacity to act: to admit refugees and organize their defense when other governments are unwilling to help. We should be able to defend that capacity without pretending a state makes its inhabitants invulnerable.

The Jewish question and Évian

The phrase the Jewish question reflects societies’ persistent uncertainty or hostility about the place Jews should occupy within them. For Jews, the practical questions concerned where they could live, work, raise children, and participate without being treated as permanent outsiders. Political Zionism sought an answer to this predicament decades before the Holocaust.11

From July 6 to 15, 1938, delegates from thirty-two countries met at Évian to discuss refugees from Germany and annexed Austria. Although persecution was already public knowledge, most participating governments declined to make substantial changes in admission policy. The United States retained its quota framework, and the Dominican Republic’s offer of resettlement did not produce an adequate general refuge.12

Refugees did reach safety in many countries, helped by people who worked to save them. The failure at Évian concerned the enormous gap between the need for refuge and the willingness of governments to provide it. It cannot support a claim that nobody helped or that every border remained completely closed.

The British White Paper of May 1939 allowed for approximately 75,000 Jewish immigrants to Palestine over five years, subject to economic conditions and administrative decisions; further immigration would depend on Arab acquiescence. Britain acknowledged Jewish distress while retaining control over admission.13 The Jewish community’s willingness to receive refugees could not override that policy.

The Arab population feared political displacement, and Jewish desperation could not remove that fear or invalidate Arab claims to a political future in their own country. Other states also had responsibilities to people seeking sanctuary. Their failure concentrated the consequences of an international refugee crisis on a small country already inhabited by another people.

Reading Jastrow after the Holocaust

Jastrow died in 1921, having developed his arguments amid the destruction of the First World War and hopes for a more democratic international order. He could not assess the Holocaust, and we should not judge him for failing to foresee its particular course. We do, however, have to assess proposals for Jewish security in light of what happened after his death.

Citizenship, minority protections, and international guarantees failed catastrophically. Anyone proposing reliance on them has to explain why similar arrangements would survive the pressure under which earlier protections collapsed. Their moral appeal cannot establish that reliability.

The Holocaust strengthens the case for a place where Jews control admission to refuge and can organize collective defense. It cannot prove that an earlier Jewish state would have prevented the genocide, since that counterfactual depends on borders, military capacities, immigration policies, international support, and the course of a war we cannot rerun.

Nor can Jewish catastrophe authorize every subsequent decision made in the name of survival. Palestinians bore no responsibility for Europe’s crimes, and the urgency of Jewish refuge could not erase their claims to their homes and political freedom. The justification for seeking a state and the justification for particular actions taken to establish or defend it have to be assessed separately.

I also see no reason for Zionists to regard flourishing diaspora communities as an embarrassment to their position. Their achievements give Jews reasons to preserve several forms of communal life and several sources of security, with an honest assessment of the vulnerabilities of each.

Nationalism and imperial sponsorship

Jastrow’s fear that nationalism could damage Jewish ethical life deserves a response even from those who reject his definition of Judaism. Jewish leaders can elevate national power above other commitments, denounce limits on their authority as betrayal, and treat another people’s claims as threats. Our history offers no immunity from those temptations.

He also recognizes that promises of formally equal rights may coexist with political domination. A Palestinian population could possess rights on paper while another group controlled the decisions governing its future. That objection concerns the distribution of power within a state, and a defense of Jewish peoplehood alone cannot answer it.2

British sponsorship exposed Jews to an imperial government whose priorities could diverge sharply from their needs, while giving Arabs reason to understand Zionism as an extension of foreign rule. The White Paper demonstrated the hazards of relying on the power whose earlier declaration Zionists had welcomed.1,13,14

Jastrow himself distinguished German militarism from forms of British imperial rule he regarded more favorably, as Shaul notes. His alternatives also depended on judgments about which powerful institutions could be trusted. Reading him as a straightforward exponent of contemporary anti-colonialism would obscure those assumptions.

An account centered exclusively on European empire also leaves out the Jewish attachment to the land that preceded modern nationalism. Political Zionism transformed that attachment through the ideas and institutions of its time. British sponsorship helps explain its political development without exhausting the reasons Jews sought a national home, just as their historical attachment cannot justify every action undertaken to establish one.

Jastrow gives us reasons to scrutinize Jewish power and impose restraints upon it. To conclude that Jews should therefore forgo sovereignty requires a further argument that some other arrangement would provide adequate protection without reproducing comparable abuses.

Jewish and Palestinian self-determination

A Palestinian reader could accept Jewish peoplehood and the reality of antisemitism while asking why Jewish self-determination should have required Palestinian dispossession. Sincere Jewish motives cannot answer that objection. Palestinian displacement in 1948, remembered as the Nakba, belongs in any assessment of the state’s establishment and its consequences.15

A national home worthy of defense must provide equality before the law and equal political rights for its citizens while accommodating the other people’s collective freedom. The need for refuge cannot indefinitely excuse denying others secure political rights. Otherwise we ask Palestinians to accept conditions of dependence that our own argument identifies as intolerable for Jews.

Alternatives to the existing arrangement need scrutiny too. Whether we consider two states, a binational state, a federation, or a confederation, we need to know who would enforce minority rights, protect people from violence, and secure consent from populations with reasons to distrust one another. A constitutional promise of equality could fail them if the institutions charged with enforcing it lacked authority or public support.

Jastrow’s proposed Palestinian state included the nationalities living there. We can examine its provisions and assumptions without projecting a contemporary Palestinian national program onto it, or assuming its moral aspirations establish its practical adequacy.2

I want Jewish and Palestinian self-determination to constrain one another: neither people’s freedom should depend on the other’s permanent political subordination. Whatever borders or institutions we favor, proposals that treat either population as removable violate that commitment.

Why recover Jastrow now?

Shaul wants us to encounter Jewish possibilities that contemporary debate has excluded. Recovering learned opposition to political Zionism challenges the claim that such opposition is necessarily outside Jewish legitimacy. It leaves us free to criticize the recovered arguments, including their definitions of Judaism and their expectations of the states under which Jews would live.1

I would ask Shaul which parts of Jastrow’s account he thinks can guide us now. His explicit disclaimer makes that an open question. Does the usefulness of the essay lie chiefly in Jastrow’s warnings about nationalism and imperial sponsorship? Does his proposal for a shared state remain persuasive after the collapse of minority protections elsewhere? And does its defense require accepting his view that Judaism has outgrown its national existence?

I would ask Shaul to put Jastrow’s premises into sustained conversation with Gottheil, Dubnow, Ahad Ha’am, and Brandeis, then follow the debate through Kaplan and the Columbus Platform. Their disagreements over Judaism also produced different judgments about citizenship, national institutions, and sovereignty. Recovering one participant should bring those objections back into the discussion too.

Judaism and the responsibilities of power

Scholars can challenge our accounts of Jewish history, and religious communities can interpret the obligations inherited from it. Neither scholarship nor theology can relieve us of deciding which political arrangements allow Jews to live securely while respecting the freedom of the people affected by those decisions.

I continue to identify as a liberal Zionist because Jewish experiences of dependence give sovereignty a powerful justification, and because exercising that sovereignty creates obligations to everyone affected by it. If control over admission, protection, and collective institutions is essential to Jewish freedom, we cannot dismiss Palestinians’ demand for control over their own future.

Kaplan’s civilization includes more than a state can organize, which gives us reasons to preserve Jewish communities and loyalties beyond Israel. Ahad Ha’am’s criticism asks us to judge political achievement by the cultural life it sustains. Jastrow’s warnings oblige us to consider the people who may bear the cost of our security. These arguments can inform our choices without supplying a single definition from which the correct political order follows automatically.

I am grateful to Shaul for returning us to these disagreements. I want to continue them with a defense of Jewish sovereignty that remains answerable to Palestinian freedom and to the Jewish commitments we expect sovereignty to serve. That requires enforceable rights, limits on government, and a willingness to change policies when they violate those commitments, including when our own leaders insist that necessity excuses them.
