Understanding the Israeli election

How the results could produce a government

A guide for the community · October 27, 2026

Israel has voted, but who will govern remains unresolved. The main election-night projections leave Benjamin Netanyahu and his established allies short of a parliamentary majority. The parties seeking to replace him have possible ways to assemble one, but they disagree about whom they are willing to govern with. [3, 4, 5]

The central question is whether those parties can turn their opposition to Netanyahu into an agreement on a government.

What Israelis actually elected

Israelis voted for the Knesset, Israel’s 120-member parliament. Each member is often called an MK, short for “member of Knesset.” Voters choose a party list, and parties receive seats broadly in proportion to their votes. A list must win at least 3.25 percent of valid votes to enter parliament. [1]

The government is the prime minister and cabinet. A coalition is a formal alliance of parties that agree to govern together. Winning the largest number of seats does not automatically make a party’s leader prime minister; the decisive task is assembling enough parliamentary support. [2]

That is why 61 appears so often in the news: it is more than half of the Knesset. A united group of 61 can command a majority even if every other member votes against it. But, as we will see, a government can sometimes take office with fewer than 61 votes.

A party keeps its Knesset seats whether it joins the coalition, supports a government from outside, opposes it, or abstains. Being outside the government does not mean being excluded from parliament.

The groups behind the numbers

For understanding this election, it helps to distinguish three groups.

Netanyahu’s bloc consists of his Likud party and its usual ultra-Orthodox and far-right partners. “Bloc” simply means a group of parties expected to work together. [3]

The Jewish-led opposition includes four principal parties:

  • Yashar, led by Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief.
  • Together, also called B’Yachad, led by former prime ministers Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid.
  • The Democrats, led by Yair Golan.
  • Yisrael Beiteinu, led by Avigdor Lieberman.

These parties span the right, center and left. Their shared opposition to Netanyahu leaves substantial disagreements over policy and potential partners. [3, 7, 8]

The Arab-led lists are Ra’am, led by Mansour Abbas, and the Joint List, an alliance of other parties. These are parties in Israel’s own parliamentary system, drawing their support mainly from Arab citizens of Israel. Their lists can also include Jewish candidates. Ra’am and the Joint List are distinct political organizations and need not make the same decisions. [3, 9]

Both oppose Netanyahu’s continuation in office. They are counted separately in the table because the other opposition parties have not agreed to work with them. [3]

These figures already include Netanyahu’s usual right-wing and religious partners. Adding those parties does not bring him to 61 in these projections. His opponents collectively have more than 61 seats, but that total includes the Arab-led lists.

Pollsters disagree considerably. Channel 14’s latest projection gives Netanyahu’s bloc 60 seats, after briefly putting it at 62. That disagreement, and the continuing count, are reasons to avoid declaring a final winner. [6]

Why cooperation with Arab parties is the central obstacle

Yair Golan has explicitly supported forming a government with Ra’am. Bennett, Eisenkot and Lieberman have resisted such a partnership; Lieberman has also explicitly rejected a minority-government arrangement. Those positions sharply restrict the opposition’s options. [7, 8]

There is a recent precedent for cooperation. Ra’am formally joined the Bennett–Lapid coalition in 2021, although it held no cabinet ministerial post. Coalition membership and cabinet membership are separate matters. [9]

The current difficulty involves disagreements over security, national identity, religion and the conditions for partnership. A shared desire to replace Netanyahu does not settle those disagreements.

A government without Lieberman is mathematically possible

Using Kan’s projection consistently, the Jewish-led opposition has 51 seats. Removing Lieberman’s eight leaves 43. Adding Ra’am’s seven and the Joint List’s eleven produces 61:

43 + 7 + 11 = 61

That combination would include Eisenkot’s Yashar, Bennett and Lapid’s Together, Golan’s Democrats, Ra’am and the Joint List. If all 61 supported the same proposed government, it could be approved even if Lieberman voted against it. [4]

The equivalent combination reaches 62 in Channel 13’s projection and 61 in Channel 12’s. This is a calculation, not an announced agreement. It requires every participating party to accept the arrangement and deliver its members’ votes. [4, 5]

A majority coalition excluding both Arab lists would require substantial support across the current blocs: the 51-member opposition would need ten additional members, perhaps through an agreement with an entire party. These are illustrations of the arithmetic, not predictions.

How abstentions could help a government take office

A proposed government normally takes office by winning a Knesset confidence vote: more members must vote for it than against it. Members who abstain vote neither yes nor no and are excluded from that comparison. [2, 10]

There is a concrete example. In June 2021, the Bennett–Lapid government was approved by 60 votes to 59, with one abstention. It did not need 61 affirmative votes. [11]

This allows a party to help a government take office without formally joining its coalition. It can vote in favor from outside, or sometimes abstain so the government’s opponents cannot outvote its supporters. A government whose coalition holds fewer than 61 seats is called a minority government.

But abstention only helps when the numbers work. Under Kan’s current projection, if the 51 Jewish-led opposition members voted yes, Netanyahu’s 51 voted no, and the 18 members of the Arab-led lists abstained, the result would be 51–51. The proposed government would fail.

If all 51 opposition members and Ra’am’s seven instead voted yes, while the Joint List’s eleven abstained, the government could pass 58–51. That example still assumes Lieberman’s participation, which he has rejected. Abstention changes the arithmetic; it does not remove the need for political agreement. [4, 8, 10]

What the leaders are saying now

Lapid has said a government might begin with a narrow coalition and expand later. That suggests possible openness to a minority arrangement, but it is not an explicit commitment to accept Arab-party support. [12]

Abbas says figures from both political blocs have approached him. He has signaled willingness to join an Eisenkot government if it can be made viable. Talks and signals of flexibility have begun; an agreement has not been announced. [13]

What happens next

The count must establish the final distribution of seats. President Isaac Herzog will consult party representatives and assign a Knesset member the task of forming a government. The largest party does not automatically receive that opportunity. The initial candidate normally has 28 days, with a possible 14-day extension; if the effort fails, other attempts can follow. If the legal process ends without a government, another election follows. [2, 3]

Meanwhile, the outgoing government continues serving until a successor takes office. Netanyahu therefore does not automatically leave office because his existing alliance falls short of a majority. [15]

A minority government would still need support to pass budgets and legislation. Israel’s rules offer some protection against removal: replacing a government through a vote of no confidence requires 61 members supporting a specific alternative government. That does not eliminate other routes to an early election. [1, 14]

The developments to watch are the final seat totals, whether parties revise their coalition commitments, and whether a proposed government can win a confidence vote. Those will determine whether Israel gets a new governing coalition, an arrangement crossing today’s political divisions, or another election.
